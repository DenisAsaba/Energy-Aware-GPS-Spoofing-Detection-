Now you deliberately make GPS imperfect.

Real GPS isn't perfectly identical to ground truth.

So create a normal GPS model first.

For example:

     ↓
Normal GPS measurement

Now create your spoofing module.

I recommend making one Python file responsible for attacks.

Something conceptually like:

Attack 1 — Position offset

(30.4580, -91.1750)
The vehicle hasn't actually moved there.

t2 → B

8. Attack 3 — Gradual drift
This is one of your most important experiments.

Instead of:

0 m
500 m
you do something like:

0 m
5 m
10 m
15 m
20 m
...
The spoofed location slowly diverges from reality.

This creates a much more interesting question:

Can the multi-sensor system detect a GPS attack that individually looks reasonable at each timestep?

This is likely one of the strongest experiments in your thesis.

9. Attack 4 — Route deviation
This one requires more work.

The attacker should make GPS suggest that the vehicle is on or approaching another legitimate road.

For example:

             Road B
            /
True EV ───A
            \
             Road C
The actual EV takes Road B.

The spoofed GPS makes the navigation system believe it is approaching Road C.

This is much more interesting than simply placing the vehicle in an impossible field.

Why?

Because:

A sophisticated attacker can generate GPS coordinates that are individually map-valid.

Your detector therefore needs temporal and physical consistency, not merely:

"Is the GPS point on a road?"

                      /

12. Then implement the Kalman Filter
Once the simple predictor works, replace/improve it with a Kalman Filter.

Conceptually:

                ┌─────────────┐
GPS ───────────►│             │
                │   Kalman    │──► Estimated position
Motion ────────►│   Filter    │
                │             │
                └─────────────┘
The filter predicts where the vehicle should be and updates that estimate using measurements.

You don't need to begin with an extremely complicated vehicle model.

A simple state might contain:

x
y
vx
vy
and later perhaps:

ax
ay
The important thing is to understand what the filter is doing rather than simply importing a library and claiming "sensor fusion."

22. Phase 10 — Design the routing attack carefully
This is another critical issue.

Simply moving the GPS location does not automatically mean the EV will consume more energy.

Your simulation needs a causal mechanism:

GPS spoofing
      ↓
navigation believes location is different
      ↓
routing algorithm chooses different route
      ↓
route has different energy characteristics
      ↓
EV consumes more energy
Therefore, you need a SUMO road network with multiple possible routes.

Ideally, construct scenarios such as:

Route A
Shorter / lower energy:

10 km
low congestion
Route B
Longer / higher energy:

14 km
more congestion
Then your spoofing scenario needs to influence the routing decision.

This is probably the hardest conceptual part of the project.

23. Don't assume spoofing automatically changes routing
This deserves emphasis.

Your research hypothesis is not:

"GPS spoofing causes energy loss."

It is closer to:

"Under an eco-routing system that depends on GPS-derived localization, certain GPS spoofing attacks can manipulate route selection and thereby increase EV energy consumption."

You need to experimentally demonstrate the intermediate steps.

25. Your train/test strategy
This is another place where projects can accidentally produce misleadingly high accuracy.

Do not simply generate 10,000 points from one trip, randomly split them, and report 99% accuracy.

Adjacent points from the same trip are highly correlated.

Instead:

Trips 1–N
    ↓
Training

Different trips/routes
    ↓
Testing
Even better:

Train:
Route A, B

Test:
Route C
and perhaps:

Train:
attack intensity 1–2

Test:
attack intensity 3
This helps demonstrate generalization.
